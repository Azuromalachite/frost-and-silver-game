Frost & Silver 1500 → 1600
In sixteenth-century Latin America, GDP per person grew. Life did not get better: people had less to eat. Play six harvests across the century and see how both can be true.
Manual: set your crews above, then press Bring in harvest.
Before you start
The question

GDP grew. Did life get better? Economic historians estimate that output per person in Latin America rose a little across the 1500s, even as the population collapsed. GDP counts everything produced, including silver shipped across the world. It does not show who ate. Through six harvests you will watch two numbers side by side: GDP per person and food per person.

You run an ayllu of about 1,000 people

Your fields climb the mountain from a warm maize valley at 1,800 m to the frozen puna at 4,300 m. Higher fields freeze more often, so spreading crews across the zones is your insurance. Each year you send about 14 work crews (fewer if people die) to plant, build terraces, or freeze-dry potatoes into chuño for your community stores.

You play six harvests, each one farming year. Harvests 1–3 are three years in a row under the Inca, around 1500. Harvests 4–6 are sample years from c. 1575, c. 1592 and c. 1600, after the Spanish colonial mita began sending one man in seven from the communities of the mita region (yours is one) to the silver mines at Potosí. Between them, decades pass. Your goal is to feed everyone 1,700 kcal a day.

Why farm the cold heights?

Each height grows something the others can't, and one frost rarely hits every level at once. A community with fields at every height eats a mixed diet and keeps eating in a bad year. The anthropologist John Murra called this the vertical archipelago.

In the game, each zone has room for only 5 crews (more once you build terraces or raised fields). So feeding everyone means sending some crews uphill, where frost is more likely.

      How the model works: which numbers carry the lesson, and which are only for play

      Carry the lesson (change these and the result can change)

      • Colonial quotas fixed at the 1573 count. The mita takes one man in seven of the 1573 population and tribute is a fixed total, so the burden per person rises as people die. This follows Toledo's system; the size of the tribute (500 kcal per person per day at the 1573 count, taken in food) is a game figure.
      • Inca labour duty: one man in ten on state and temple fields, half of that harvest returned as feasts and rations. Teaching assumption; how much came back is debated. The game also leaves out exchange: Andean communities traded and used markets as well as relying on redistribution (Hirth & Pillsbury 2013), so the Inca years here are simpler than the real economy.
      • Land per person: output in a zone grows with labour to the power 0.85, so survivors of an epidemic each farm a little more land. The exponent is close to 1 because terraces need labour to keep up (Wernke 2010 describes terraces abandoned as people were lost). Teaching assumption.
      • Resettlement: Toledo's reducciones moved families into nucleated towns, often far from their fields (Wernke 2010). The change came gradually as distant fields and terraces were given up, and it varied from place to place, with some communities negotiating to keep their land (Hunter 2025; de la Puente Luna & Perales 2026). The game lowers colonial yields by 7% at c. 1575, 12% at c. 1592 and 15% at c. 1600; the sizes are teaching assumptions.
      • Population: a 50% fall before 1573 and 15% more in the 1585–91 epidemics. Historians' estimates vary widely; these are round game figures.
      • Silver versus food in GDP: GDP per person = 340 + 0.045 × field output + silver. A mine worker's silver counts somewhat more than a farm worker's output. Teaching assumptions, tuned so the Inca years start near Maddison's 416; the "reverse" panel shows what happens if they are different.

      Only for play (they change single years, not the lesson)

      • Zone yields, frost odds and frost losses, drought odds and size, the Pleiades forecast's accuracy (the forecasting practice itself is real: Orlove, Chiang & Cane 2000).
      • Terrace and raised-field effects. Raised fields protect against frost through their water and shape, and how much depends on the field (Kolata & Ortloff 1989), so the game gives waru waru more frost protection than terraces; the sizes are play values. Also room per zone, chuño capacity, the starting community stores, the 1,900 kcal eating cap and spoilage.
      • Deaths from hunger, the wage crews' tribute relief, and the 30% household spread behind "households below 1,700".

The original author built the game around one message: GDP per person grew in sixteenth-century Latin America, but life did not get better, because food per person fell. He drew on Maddison's historical statistics and his familiarity with global economic history, and also used the game to teach the quipu.

      The game is a model, not a reconstruction. Anchors: GDP per person of 416 in 1500 and 438 in 1600 are Maddison's conjectural estimates for Latin America as a whole (1990 international dollars; not Andes-specific), and the game's GDP formula was tuned to start near 416. The fall from about 1,700 to about 1,200 kcal is the original game's teaching figure and has no source we could confirm. Here “kcal per person per day” means average food available across all ages, capped at 1,900. The household share assumes household food varies 30% around the average; it is a guess that illustrates the point Wernke & Whitmore (2009, Human Ecology 37:421) make, that households can go short while a region has a surplus. Every other number is the game's own. For scale, adults in a modern multi-country sample that excluded manual labourers used about 2,190 (women) and 2,720 (men) kcal a day (Pontzer et al. 2016, Nature 533:390, Table 1); a community average that includes children is lower.
